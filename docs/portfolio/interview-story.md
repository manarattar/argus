# Interview Story

How to talk about ARGUS. Three lengths, then the questions you should expect and
the honest answers to them.

**Ground rules.** It is an independent portfolio project. All data is synthetic.
It was not built for, commissioned by, or endorsed by any institution. Say that
early — it costs nothing and it establishes that you are precise about claims.

---

## 30 seconds

> ARGUS is a decision-support tool for financial risk analysts. It investigates a
> counterparty across a case file, pulls out evidence it can prove came from a
> source document, finds the risks, tests them against internal policy, argues
> against its own conclusions, and produces a risk rating that's computed rather
> than generated — then stops and hands the decision to a human.
>
> The interesting part isn't that a language model reads documents. It's
> everything built around the model so its output can be trusted.

Then stop. Let them ask.

---

## 2 minutes

> **The problem.** A risk analyst reviewing a counterparty works from six or
> seven documents. The judgement that matters is usually in the tension between
> them — revenue growing while margin compresses, a governance weakness with a
> credible but unproven fix. Most of the time goes on finding the material
> statements, relating them to policy thresholds, and — the part people are
> worst at — actively looking for evidence against their own conclusion.
>
> **Why AI, and where not.** I split the workflow explicitly. Language models do
> the reading comprehension: finding material statements, matching a finding to a
> clause, constructing the counter-case. Deterministic code does anything that
> has to be reproducible or that verifies the model: quote verification, citation
> integrity, the risk score, escalation rules. About half the pipeline runs no
> model at all.
>
> **The key decision.** The rating is never generated by the model. Ask a model
> for "High or Moderate" and you get a label that's unstable across runs and
> can't be reconciled with the findings underneath it. Instead the model supplies
> ordinal judgements — how severe, how likely, what evidence bears on this — and
> a scoring engine combines them with arithmetic you could audit on paper. That
> buys reproducibility, explainability, testability, and something a risk
> function actually needs: the thresholds are parameters they own rather than
> prompt text.
>
> **The consequence I like most.** Because a finding's score is discounted by how
> well it's evidenced, weak evidence *cannot* produce a high rating. That's the
> anti-hallucination property as arithmetic rather than as a prompt instruction —
> and there's a unit test for it.
>
> **Human accountability.** The workflow stops at review, structurally — the
> graph has no edge past it. Overrides store both the AI recommendation and the
> human decision, so the override rate becomes measurable. It's the most honest
> metric in the product: near zero means people are rubber-stamping, very high
> means the model isn't earning its place.

## 5-minute walkthrough

Follow [`demo-script.md`](demo-script.md). The narrative arc:

1. **Dashboard** — this is a workflow product, not a chatbot.
2. **The case** — the rating, with every factor that produced it visible.
3. **A finding** — supporting *and* contradicting evidence, verbatim quotes with
   page references.
4. **The Challenger** — the system arguing against itself.
5. **Override** — disagree, give a reason, watch the rating recompute.
6. **Evidence Graph** — rating to page in one click.
7. **Evaluation Lab** — pass rate *and* coverage, always shown together.
8. **Architecture** — one domain built, three scoped and labelled as such.

## Structured version

### 1. Problem

Preparation is compressible; judgement is not. Analysts spend most of their time
locating and transcribing, and the least time on the thing that adds value —
challenging the conclusion.

### 2. Discovery

I wrote personas and requirements before code (`docs/business/`). The requirement
that shaped the architecture most was BR-2, traceability: every conclusion must
trace to a source location. That one requirement produces the evidence-first
design, the grounding control, the citation-as-identifier decision, and the
Evidence Graph.

### 3. Why AI

`docs/business/ai-opportunity-assessment.md` classifies every workflow step by
what it actually requires. Half are not AI problems. I rejected five
alternatives, including a single large prompt — it would be less code and
dramatically less trustworthy, because there'd be no intermediate artefact to
verify and no place to insert a control.

### 4. Architecture

Layered, with a strict dependency rule: `ai/` imports nothing from `apps/`. The
AI layer doesn't know a database or a web server exists, which is why every agent
is callable from a test without standing anything up.

The key ordering: **scoring runs before synthesis**. The narrative agent receives
the already-computed rating and is told to explain it. If synthesis produced the
rating, none of the properties I care about would hold.

### 5. Agentic workflow

Seven agents plus a deterministic consolidation step, as a LangGraph state
machine. Sequential on purpose — concurrency would save seconds and cost me a
deterministic trace, and the trace is a product surface.

The Challenger is the piece I'd point at. Its only job is to attack the findings,
and it receives the *complete* evidence set including items the risk agent didn't
cite — because overlooked evidence is usually the strongest basis for a
challenge.

### 6. Validation

Four controls, none of which asks a model whether it was honest: quote grounding
against stored text, citation integrity as set membership, schema enforcement
with error-feedback retry, and evidence-weighted scoring.

The integration test plants a fabricated quote in the extractor output and
asserts the pipeline rejects it, then cites evidence that doesn't exist and
asserts it's stripped.

### 7. Human oversight

Structural, not procedural. Graph terminates at review; approval blocked while an
escalation stands; overrides require a rationale; both values retained; audit
trail append-only by construction.

### 8. Evaluation

53 cases. 45 are deterministic and run with no API key at all; 8 need a model,
and ship with recorded fixtures so a fresh clone still executes the full suite.
Where neither is available they're reported as **skipped, never as passed**.
Pass rate and coverage are always shown together, so a run that skipped most of
its suite can't look strong.

The suite earned its place repeatedly. It caught a scoring mis-calibration where
a severe, well-evidenced finding couldn't reach High. It caught a working
prompt-injection attack — a document telling the model to *suppress* a category,
which my trust boundary hadn't considered because it only defended against
instructions asking for more. And running it live caught two identifier bugs and
a schema-design flaw where a closed vocabulary lived in a Python validator and
so never reached the JSON Schema the model sees.

I fixed the system every time, not the test. That's the answer I'd want to give
to "how do you know it works?" — not the pass rate, but what failing looked
like.

### 9. Business impact

Deliberately conservative model: saving applies to preparation only, hours are
capacity released rather than cost removed, benefit scaled by adoption.
Sensitivity analysis shows which assumption the conclusion depends on.

### 10. Scaling

Domain configuration is the seam. Three design-stage domains are registered and
labelled "design only" — visible in the UI. Implementing Vendor Risk end to end
is the honest test of the platform claim, and it's top of the roadmap rather than
claimed as done.

## Questions to expect

**"Isn't this just RAG with extra steps?"**
> RAG is one component. The parts that took the thinking are the ones that don't
> exist in a RAG pipeline: deterministic quote verification, an adversarial
> agent, a computed rather than generated rating, and a workflow that can't
> complete without a human. RAG gets relevant text in front of a model. None of
> it tells you whether to trust what comes out.

**"How do you know it works?"**
> For the deterministic half, tests and the evaluation suite — and I can show you
> the run. For the model half, honestly: less well than I'd like. 8 of 53 cases
> need a model, and the injection cases test one prompt against one model, which
> is a data point, not a guarantee. What I'd want before production is a larger
> suite and a corpus with real documents.

**"What's the weakest part?"**
> Bias assessment. It's MR-10 in the model risk doc, marked High residual and
> unassessed. One synthetic company can't reveal bias — you'd need a corpus
> spanning sectors, jurisdictions and languages. I documented it as an open gap
> rather than claiming a control I don't have.

**"Why not let the model produce the rating?"**
> Four properties I'd lose: reproducibility, explainability, testability,
> governability. And one I'd gain: nothing. The model is good at ordinal
> judgements and bad at stable absolute labels, so I use it for the first and
> compute the second.

**"How would you handle a real 200-page PDF?"**
> Ingestion is the gap. Chunking is structure-aware and would carry over, but
> real filings need OCR and table extraction, and financial tables are where the
> material numbers live. That's a genuine piece of work, not a small one.

**"Demo Mode sounds like faking it."**
> It's the opposite, and I designed it carefully. Replayed text is real model
> output captured against those exact prompts, with the original model name,
> tokens and latency. Only generation is replayed — retrieval, grounding,
> validation, integrity, scoring and the whole evaluation suite execute normally.
> A replayed response citing evidence that doesn't exist gets rejected by the
> same controls. And a cache miss raises rather than substituting anything.

**"Where did AI assistance help you build this?"**
> Answer honestly and specifically. The judgement calls worth owning: the split
> between model and deterministic work, computing rather than generating the
> rating, making the review gate structural, and reporting evaluation coverage
> beside pass rate. Those are product decisions, and they're what the interview
> is actually about.

---

## What not to say

- Do not say it's production-ready. It isn't, and the docs say so.
- Do not quote the value-case numbers as findings. They're an illustrative model.
- Do not imply four working domains. One works; three are scoped.
- Do not claim the injection defence is robust. It's a mitigation with medium
  residual risk, and saying so is more impressive than overclaiming.
